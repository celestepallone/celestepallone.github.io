Dr. Celeste T. Pallone

Paleoceanographer & Isotope Geochemist  |  Postdoctoral Associate  |  Department of Earth, Atmospheric & Planetary Sciences  |  Massachusetts Institute of Technology

Google Scholar · ORCID · Bluesky · CV · LinkedIn

I use proxy records derived from marine sediments to characterize gradual, cyclical, and abrupt changes in the Earth's climate and ocean systems throughout the Cenozoic. I earned a BA in Environmental Science from Barnard College in 2019 and a Ph.D. in Earth and Environmental Sciences from Columbia University in 2025, advised by Jerry McManus. I am currently a postdoc in the McGee Lab for Paleoclimate and Geochronology at MIT.

Research

Pleistocene Tropical Pacific Paleoceanography

The tropical Pacific is the dominant source of sub-decadal climate variability today, but how ocean-atmosphere dynamics in the eastern equatorial Pacific (EEP) may change with future climate is still uncertain. Paleoceanographic records are historically complicated by spatial heterogeneity and proxy biases, resulting in longstanding debates on productivity dynamics and the region’s role in global carbon cycling. My Ph.D. was a multi-proxy study of key elements of the tropical Pacific ocean-atmosphere system over the last glacial and interglacial periods, including EEP thermocline depth, carbon cycling, and sea surface temperature variability.

International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP) Expedition 397: Iberian Margin Paleoclimate

Deep-sea sediments from the Iberian Margin are unique paleoclimate archives, containing millennial-scale proxies of North Atlantic and Antarctic temperatures correlatable to high latitude ice cores and proxies that reflect the distribution and ventilation rates of deep-water masses. I was a sedimentologist on IODP Expedition 397 in 2022 and helped recover, characterize, and catalogue over 6 km of deep-sea sediments along a depth transect of four southwest Iberian Margin sites that were deposited across the Pleistocene, Pliocene, and Miocene. I am working with a team of students from Columbia University and collaborating with my Ph.D. advisor Dr. Jerry McManus to generate a benthic foraminifera stable isotope record across the last 1.5 Ma at Iberian Margin Site U1587 (3479 meters below sea level [mbsl]).

Eocene Hyperthermal Events

Rapid excursions from equilibrium climate states occurred throughout the Cenozoic, but the most relevant analogues for modern and future climate change may come from the hothouse climate of the early Eocene, in which global temperatures were warmer than the present, atmospheric CO2 concentrations were higher, and global ice volume was minimal. My ongoing postdoctoral research focuses on early Eocene hyperthermal events, which were transient, less than 100 kyr warming events that occurred with a rapid addition of carbon to the ocean-atmosphere system. I am characterizing changes in ocean carbon cycling in the deep equatorial Atlantic across hyperthermals using redox-sensitive minor and trace elements in deep-sea sediments.
